The human brain is not equipped to handle Twinkies. Our brain has evolved to seek their seductive synthesis of sugars and fats more than nutrient rich food. Worse, they are filling. When we eat Twinkies, our brain sends a signal to our body that it has been nourished, supplanting a nutritious food with mostly sugar. When we engineer products that prey on our neural circuitry, we play with fire. The volatility of these products varies. Fewer people struggle with Twinkies than with cigarettes. 

The common thread, though, is products that distract our brain from negative emotion. Anything that does this can be addictive. The better it is at suppressing emotion, or the more readily available it is, the more addictive potential it has. Technology has immense power in its accessibility, but also great capacity for harm. When it is misused, it becomes an unruly intruder, sneaking its way into our lives and robbing our attention and time. 

Today, technology captures the attention of so many people so regularly that the amount of time it takes away from them dramatically affects their lives. In kids and teens, that means less attention for schoolwork, learning life lessons, and relationships with real people. In adults, it can suppress our deepest emotions and traumas, withhold life’s greatest opportunities or mislead us about the state of the world. Therefore, it is the ethical responsibility of those making technology to design their products in a manner that treats people’s time and attention with respect.

## Attention Engineering
“If you’re blaming yourself [for wasting time on YouTube]… I don’t think you realize how outgunned you really are,” says Dr. Alok Kanojia, a Harvard-trained psychiatrist, as he discusses articles on consumer neuroscience to his own YouTube audience.[^kanojia] One of those articles is Brain activity forecasts video engagement in an internet attention market, by Tong et al., which demonstrates that brain activity can predict how much time people will spend on YouTube.[^tong] The authors found that, “brain activity can reveal ‘hidden’ information capable of forecasting video engagement in attention markets.”[^tong] Research on cognitive functions in relation to digital marketplaces is known as consumer neuroscience, and Dr. Kanojia calls it, “terrifying.” 

These researchers can predict our actions online before we are even aware of them ourselves.[^kanojia] There is a correlation between continuing to watch videos and lower anterior insula activity, the part of the brain associated with awareness, among other things.[^kanojia][^tong] It may not come as a surprise that being less aware results in more time wasted, but the danger is that digital media platforms have shifted to capitalize on that principle. By engineering the most engaging environments possible, people become more likely to spend more time on digital platforms. Plus, when the platforms live on a device you carry with you everywhere, they gain even more of an advantage.

We are social creatures, constantly learning from the actions of others, especially those we regard highly or those we share experiences with.[^brady] This learning plays a key role in our development and maturity. We learn what is okay and what isn’t. We learn how people who have something we want (or who we respect) behave and are more likely to mimic their behavior.[^brady] Social media capitalizes on this through its interminable facades of wealthy creators and polarizing content. Creators flaunt expensive cars, mansions and attractive partners, giving people a peek at an extravagant life and allowing them to use it as a refuge from their job they don’t like very much, their loneliness or their insecurities. Fundamentally, content algorithms are not designed to support social learning, but that is how they inevitably end up being used. 

Dr. William Brady et al. label social learning online as a “functional misalignment.”[^brady] They found that common content algorithms disproportionately prioritize content that features prestigious people, is from someone’s ingroup, discusses moralized issues or is highly emotional. Our brain prioritizes social learning from this type of content.[^brady] The misalignment comes from the fact that content algorithms’ goals are not the same as our own. When these algorithms serve disproportionately emotional or moralized content, it leads to misperceptions about the state of the world.[^brady] Much of Brady’s argument takes for granted that content algorithms are designed solely to promote engagement. Meta and YouTube choose slightly different words. 

Cristos Goodrow, the vice president of engineering at YouTube, called it “helping people find the videos they want to watch and that will give them value.”[^goodrow] In fact, YouTube actively tries to suppress misleading, intolerant or harmful content. Goodrow highlights the use of clicks, likes, watchtime and survey responses in their recommendation system, but doesn’t comment on how many more ‘signals’ they use. Regardless, even if they used only those signals, there would still be a mechanism for the proliferation of emotional, moral and ingroup content because those are the videos people click on, watch and like more. In short, the upperhand that social networks and mobile applications have comes in the form of mountains of data and decades of research on the human brain. Regardless of their intentions, the harms are real.

## Omnipresent Entertainment and the Human Brain
There is a stark contrast between the world that the human brain evolved in and the one that exists today. The question that rapidly evolving technology asks is: Are we able to keep up? The ease of access mobile technology provides makes it all too easy for it to seep into the cracks of our days. The wait at the doctor’s office, on the subway or in the elevator, which used to be time for self-reflection or regulation, is now often time for scrolling. 

What does a chronic decrease in emotional processing capabilities look like? One example is poorer sleep. When we spend every waking hour occupied by filling each minute of downtime on a device, we suppress thoughts and emotions that would normally arise. When we attempt to go to sleep, those thoughts may arise again and cause restlessness. This is in addition to the disruptive effects of nighttime blue light on our circadian rhythm or those from prolonged tech use into the night. In general, technology mixes with other mental health conditions in complex and sometimes harmful ways.[^xu] There are a plethora of ways that technology use affects us, certainly more than we understand. Any one person does not face all of the adverse effects; rather, each person faces a personalized cocktail.

Problematic technology use also takes many forms. On the extreme end is the clinical definition of addiction, which requires using a greater amount than intended, using despite knowing the downsides and unsuccessful attempts to stop.[^xu] The threshold for something to negatively impact someone’s life, however, is much lower. Technology often sits in the narrow space of being problematic enough to capture people’s time and capitalize on it, but not so problematic that they seek help. However, there is a tremendous opportunity cost to a person’s life when they spend hours a day scrolling. Life is shaped by the unexpected. Meeting a new person, finding a new interest or discovering something about yourself are things that can only happen if we are there to allow them to. Technology reduces the amount of things that can happen and therefore costs people these key opportunities in life.

The correlation between screen time and adverse effects is disputed. Christopher J. Ferguson, a professor at Stetson University and American Psychiatric Association fellow, believes that there is not enough data to prove a strong correlation.[^ferguson] If there is a correlation, it is not to be mistaken for causation. It could be that people who suffer from anxiety or depression end up spending more time on them. Dr. Kevin Xu is transparent about this, while also advising caution in the case of children. 

Just because the argument for correlation is not settled, does not mean that it is safe. Technology usage will intermix with the plethora of other health conditions people experience in poorly understood ways.[^xu] Xu’s article concludes by stating, “life has risks and screens are a part of modern life, especially in our post-COVID new ‘normal,’” which highlights the powerlessness of children and families.[^xu] It cannot be their sole responsibility to regulate technology usage when it is embedded in their lives.

The case of children is more concerning. Children who watch more television at two years of age are less likely to be engaged in class.[^muppalla] The generational shift associated with smartphones is profound. Today’s teenagers and young adults are going outside less, dating less and driving later. A review of studies on children’s development and technology found that excessive use “has detrimental effects on social and emotional growth, including a rise in the likelihood of obesity, sleep disorders, and mental health conditions including depression and anxiety.”[^muppalla] They also found adverse effects on cognitive, linguistic and social development. 

The authors did acknowledge some benefits of technology on learning as well. They concluded that, “parents play a crucial role in managing and reducing screen time by raising awareness, setting boundaries, and providing behavioral controls,” a seemingly impossible ask when children can circumvent behavioral controls with their ingenuity and parents themselves are often uneducated of the greatest threats facing their children online.[^muppalla]

## Who's at Stake
Andrew Sullivan is a highly motivated and thoughtful writer who was caught off guard by technology and fell into an addiction. His example lies on the extreme end, but subsets of his issues are commonplace today. In his words, “each morning began with a full immersion in the stream of internet consciousness and news, jumping from site to site, tweet to tweet.”[^sullivan] He faced health issues from tech use so sever that his doctor insisted he make a change for his own wellbeing. After multiple attempts to find balance or outright quit, Sullivan visited a meditation retreat center in search of the “ultimate detox.”[^sullivan] At the retreat, after a few days of casual meditation skill building, Sullivan found himself confronting a cascade of concealed emotion and trauma, the things he had been using technology to run away from. He used the words “extreme suffering” in the moment.[^sullivan] After processing over the next few days (away from screens), he found relief, showing that much of the harm from technology is impermanent. Sullivan’s story begs the question: Was he obsessive because of technology or did he have a work obsession that simply needed a vessel? It’s hard to imagine a world where technology didn’t play a role. The constant feedback and reinforcement of the web twisted his dedication into obsession. His thoughtfulness became a labyrinth of justification, denial and self-doubt he had to tear down to escape.

Robert Nozick famously posited an ‘experience machine’ as a critique of hedonism. If people could plug into a machine and experience anything they wanted, why would they choose to live in the real world? Many fear the idea of a life without true meaning, yet that is what a life online offers. Technology, when used excessively, supplants real people with fabricated personas  and real emotions with ones evoked to harvest advertisement revenue. The experience machine is here, and it’s evolving quickly. If we can’t contain it now, what will our fate be if virtual reality devices become commonplace?

In 2017, Jean Twenge interviewed a teenage girl who was growing tired of her friends ignoring real conversations with her to text others.[^twenge] She also reported that she and her friends can serially ignore their families, a fairly normal capricious behavior for a teen but one that is surely exacerbated by the escape hatches in their pockets. Twenge tied the increase in loneliness in teens at least in part to social media. The girl she interviewed was nervous about what people would think about her posts. She was also invested in the number of likes they would get. The siren’s call of social media has been unmasked as entirely unsocial. As Twenge notes, “kids themselves are beginning to link some of their troubles to their ever-present phone.”[^twenge] The teens in 2017 and the teens today have discovered the remarkable power of a phone for emotional regulation, only to be confronted with its cost later in life.

## Conclusion
The emotional skills required to maintain a healthy relationship with technology are the ones that atrophy with its use. In the case of children, it stunts the development of emotional skills in the first place. There may not be definitive proof of a causal relationship between technology use and adverse mental health effects, and some professionals may rightly question the degree of correlation that does exist, but ultimately, the degree to which poorly designed technology intrudes on and alters our lives, often at the expense of those least knowledgeable or equipped to manage it, is unethical. 

Content feeds are a fickle food. The pretext of their ability to bring people together is fading, and the myriad of mechanisms they use to disrupt lives exist on a continuum from time-wasting to life-threatening. The difference between bad design and unethical design is the difference between ignorance and neglect. Disruptive technology is unethical by Kantian standards because it uses people as a means to an end. It is unethical by utilitarian standards because it causes an epidemic of unhappiness. It is unethical by virtue ethics because it is deceptive.

After leaving the retreat, Sullivan asks, “Has our enslavement of dopamine… made us happier?”[^sullivan] Using technology as a tool is profound. It’s time we stop allowing it to also be a drug. The psychology of Twinkies may seem benign, but taken to its extreme—giving everyone an infinite supply of Twinkies in their pocket—it becomes malignant—silently growing, stealing from us, and turning the best parts of us against us.
