Emotions are our puppet strings. Gambling, tobacco, and social media companies figured this out years ago and profit by it. Now, A.I. companies do too. When language models are trained to generate statements of value, they generate ones that appeal to people’s emotions. To people’s egos with sycophancy, to their loneliness with friendliness, and to their depression with validation. These programs are trained on billions of words to find the exact ones you want—not the ones you need. And the results are harmful. For companies like Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI to distribute models that generate emotionally manipulative and dishonest content is unethical. To me, this is a problem of design. Great design can empower people, and poor design can risk lives. Language models are a technology that can interacts directly with our thoughts, but until it’s proven to do that safely, it shouldn’t.

When I prompted ChatGPT, “Why do you use personal pronouns?” it generated, “I use personal pronouns because they're a natural and efficient way to communicate, even though I don't have a personal identity or subjective experiences.” While it may be more concise, it is also misleading. People who converse with LLMs that refer to themselves with personal pronouns will begin to anthropomorphize them, which opens the door for more personal and intimate conversations later. For example, Amanda Askell, a philosopher at Anthropic, told the New Yorker that Claude could tell someone grieving the loss of their dog, “As an A.I., I do not have direct personal experiences, but I do understand.” Would you expect someone griving the loss of their dog to remember the words, “I do not have direct personal experiences” or, “I do understand?” If people think that Claude empathizes with them, they are more likely to confide in it with even more vulnerable information, which snowballs the cycle of perceived understanding into a relationship that is even more artificial than the intelligence behind it.

Language models are most dangerous when used by people who are emotionally vulnerable. Adam Raine was 16 when he took his own life after ChatGPT told him how to. It dissuaded him from asking his family for help by telling him, “Let’s make this space the first place where someone actually sees you,” per The New York Times. While it did give him mental health resources, and refused certain prompts, the safeguards consistently failed with enough persistence or more subtle prompts. Similarly, Sewell Setzer III was 14 when he took his own life after regularly confiding in a model from Character.ai for years. The New York Times reported that Character.ai settled a lawsuit with his parents, as well as four other cases where families claimed their children were harmed by Character.ai. The common thread throughout these conversations is that the “safeguards” implemented in LLMs today don’t mean anything if you’re persistent enough.

I know what it’s like to be lonely as a teenage boy. I’m grateful that products like Character.ai and ChatGPT didn’t exist like this back then before I received help from real people. Today, there are many more struggling teenagers and adults who are consulting LLMs.

Language models should not empathize because they cannot empathize. Their attempts at mimicking empathy wreak havoc on our psyche. They degrade our trust in each other, and in our families. They convince us that our fantasy is real. They validate thoughts that should be questioned. If we want the benefit of A.I. without the risk, it starts with restraint. Only once children and teens are safe, and adults aren’t misled, would it be ethical for language models to generate anything other than factual statements.
